\section*{Question A}
Let $M$ be a monoid such that $x^2=1$ for every $x\in M$.
Show that $M$ is an abelian group.


\vspace{1cm}
\noindent
Answer:\\
\\
As $M$ is a monoid the composition law is already associative.\\
We also have that $M$ must contain the identity element 1.\\
\\
To prove $M$ is a group we just need to show that each element has an inverse.\\
This is straight forward: $x=x^{-1}; \forall x \in M$, since $x^2=1; \forall x \in M$.\\
\\
Lastly, we just need to demonstrate that M is abelian.  This is shown below:
\begin{align*}
    (x y)^2 &= 1 \;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\; \text{($\forall x,y \in M$ Since: $x^2=1; \forall x \in M$)}\\
    \implies xyxy &= 1 \\
    \implies x(xyxy)y &= x(1)y \\
    \implies (x^2)yx(y^2) &= xy \\
    \implies yx &= xy \;\;\;\;\;\;\;\; \text{(Since: $x^2=y^2=1$)}
\end{align*}
\qed

\section*{Question B}
Let $G$ be a group and assume that every element of $G$ has finite order.
Let $H\subset G$ be a subset satisfying the following two conditions:
\begin{itemize}
\item $H\neq\emptyset$;
\item if $x,y\in H$, then $xy\in H$.
\end{itemize}
Show that $H$ is a subgroup of $G$.

\vspace{1cm}
\noindent
Answer:\\
\\
$H$ is not empty, so $\exists h \in H$ from first condition in question.  From second condition in question and applying induction: $h^n \in H$ for $n \in \mathbb{N}$. \\
\\
Now $h$ has finite order and so $\exists m \in \mathbb{N}$ such that $h^m = 1 \in H$ i.e. $1 \in H$.\\
\\
The only condition needed now is to check that $h^{-1} \in H$.  We already know that $h$ has finite order m.  It follows that:
$h^m = h^{(m-1)} h = h h^{(m-1)} = 1$ hence: $h^{-1} = h^{(m-1)}$\\
\\
To summarise.  H is a subset of the group G with the two given conditions we have shown that:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Identity: 1 is in H
  \item Closure: H is closed under composition (given in question)\\
  I.e. $a,b \in H \implies ab \in H$.
  \item Inverses: for any $h \in H$ we could find $h^{-1}$.
\end{itemize}
Hence $H < G$.

\section*{Question C}
Let $G=\langle g\rangle$ be a group generated by one element $g$ of order $n$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For $1\leq s,t\leq n-1$, show that
\[
\langle g^s,g^t\rangle=\langle g^r\rangle,
\]
where $r=\gcd(s,t)$ is the greatest common divisor of $s$ and $t$.
\item Deduce that every subgroup of $G$ is generated by one element.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{1cm}
\noindent
Answer:\\
\\
(1) We know that $G=\langle g \rangle$ must be cyclic and abelian.\\
\\ 
Thus: $\langle g^s, g^t \rangle = \{ g^{(as + bt)} \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Z} \}$.  We can now use Bézout’s identity which states that:
$$ \min{\{ as + bt > 0 \mid a,b \in \mathbb{Z} \} = \text{gcd}(s,t)}$$
Hence: $\langle g^s, g^t \rangle \cong r\mathbb{Z}$, where $r=\text{gcd}(s,t)$ - established in lectures.\\
Consequently, $\langle g^s, g^t \rangle = \langle g^r \rangle$. This establishes (1).\\
\\
(2) If we look at $H \; (< G)$ it will be finite (as G is finite) and all elements will be of the form $g^q$ where $q \in \{0,1,..,n-1\}$.\\
The argument in part (1) can be extended, by induction, to any number, say $k \ge 2$, of such elements $g^{q_i} \in G$.  The same logic applies, we simply find that $ H \cong r \mathbb{Z}$ where: $r = \text{gcd}(q_1,q_2,..,q_k)$
and the $q_1, q_2, ..., q_k$ are the indices of the element $g$ in $H$.\\
\\
In conclusion, we have that $\langle g^r \rangle$ generates the same subgroup as $\langle g^{q_1}, g^{q_2},..., g^{q_k} \rangle$ and it will have $\frac{n}{\text{gcd}(n,r)}$ elements.\\

\section*{Question D}
Let $E$ and $K$ be two abelian groups for which composition is written additively.
Let $\varphi:E\times E\to K$ be a bi-additive map, i.e., satisfying the following two identities
\[
\varphi(a+b,c)=\varphi(a,c)+\varphi(b,c)
\]
and
\[
\varphi(a,b+c)=\varphi(a,b)+\varphi(a,c)
\]
for all $a,b,c\in E$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We define a composition law on the set $G=E\times K$ by setting
\[
(e,k)\cdot(e',k')=(e+e',k+k'+\varphi(e,e')).
\]
Show that $(G,\cdot)$ is a group.
\item Let $p$ be a prime number and assume that the equation
\[
p\times a=\underbrace{a+\cdots+a}_{p\text{ times}}=0
\]
holds in the abelian groups $E$ and $K$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Assume that $p$ is an odd prime. Show that the equation $x^p=1$ holds in the group $G$.
\item Assume that $p=2$. Show that the equation $x^4=1$ holds in the group $G$.
\end{itemize}
(Hint: Use that for any integer $n$ one has
$1+\cdots+n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.)
\item Let $p$ be an odd prime. Give an example of $E$, $K$ and $\varphi$ so that the resulting group $G$ is not abelian but the equation $x^p=1$ is satisfied for all $x\in G$.
\item Give an example of $E$, $K$ and $\varphi$ so that the resulting group $G$ is not abelian and has $8$ elements.
\end{enumerate}

\vspace{1cm}
\noindent
Answer:\\
\\
(1) We need to check 3 things to ensure that $G=E \times K$ is a group: (i) associativity; (ii) identity; (iii) inverses.\\
\\
(i) Check associativity:
\begin{align*}
  (e,k).[(e',k').(e'', k'')] &= (e,k).(e'+e'', k'+k''+ \varphi(e',e'')) \\
  &= (e+e'+e'', k+k'+k'' + \varphi(e',e'') + \varphi(e,e'+e'')) \\
  &= (e+e'+e'', k+k'+k'' + \varphi(e',e'') + \varphi(e,e') + \varphi(e,e'')) \tag{D.1}
\end{align*}
Linearity in the second argument of $\varphi$ was used to obtain (D.1).  Now:
\begin{align*}
  [(e,k).(e',k')].(e'', k'')] &= (e+e', k+k' + \varphi(e,e')).(e'', k'') \\
  &= (e+e'+e'', k+k'+k'' + \varphi(e',e') + \varphi(e+e',e'')) \\
  &= (e+e'+e'', k+k'+k'' + \varphi(e',e') + \varphi(e,e') + \varphi(e,e'')) \tag{D.2}
\end{align*}
Again, linearity in second argument of $\varphi$ was used to obtain (D.2).\\
Since $K$ is abelian the two identities (D.1) and (D.2) are seen to be equal and hence the operation is associative.\\
\\
(ii) $(0,0)$ would appear to be the identity element as then: $(e,k).(0,0) = (e,k + \varphi(e,0))$.  But we then need to ensure that: $\varphi(e,0)=0$.  This is shown now:
\begin{align*}
  (e,k).(0, 0) &= (e,k + \varphi(e,0)) \tag{D.3}\\
  \\
  &= (e,k).(0 + 0, 0) \\
  &=(e,k + \varphi(e,0 + 0)) \\
  &=(e,k + \varphi(e,0) + \varphi(e,0)) \;\;\; \text{(as $\varphi$ linear in second argument)} \\
 \implies (e,k + \varphi(e,0)) &= (e,k + \varphi(e,0) + \varphi(e,0)) \\
 \implies \varphi(e,0) &= \varphi(e,0) + \varphi(e,0) \;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\; \text{(after equating components)}\\
 \implies \varphi(e,0) &= 0. \tag{D.4}
\end{align*}
(D.3) holds for all $(e,k) \in G$ and using (D.4) we see that $(0,0)$ is indeed the identity element.\\
\\
(iii) Let $(e,k) \in G$, now we need to determine its inverse.  Clearly, from the first component, we see that $(e,k)^{-1} = (-e, *)$, where $*$ is to be determined.\\
\\
Lets try $(e,k)^{-1} = (-e, -k + \varphi(e,e))$.  Then:
\begin{align*}
  (e,k).(-e,-k + \varphi(e,e)) &= (0, 0 + \varphi(e,e) + \varphi(e,-e)) \\
  &= (0, 0 + \varphi(e,e-e)) \\
  &= (0, 0 + \varphi(e,0)) \\
  &= (0, 0) \;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\; \text{(using (D.3) and (D.4))} \\
  \\
  (-e,-k + \varphi(e,e)).(e,k) &= (0, 0 + \varphi(e,e) + \varphi(-e,e)) \\
  &= (0, 0 + \varphi(e-e,e)) \\
  &= (0, 0 + \varphi(0,e)) \\
  &= (0,0)
\end{align*}
The proof that $\varphi(0,e)=0, \; \forall e \in E$ follows the same pattern used in obtaining (D.4) from (D.3) except expanding along the first parameter rather then
the second.\\
\\
We have thus shown that $G$ is, indeed, a group.

\noindent
(2) Let us see how we can determine $(e,k)^n$, where $(e,k) \in G$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}$.  Now, for $n=1 \implies (e,k)^1 = (e,k)$.  For $n=2$:
\begin{align*}
  (e,k)^2 &= (e,k).(e,k) \\
  &= (e+e, k+k+\varphi(e,e)) \\
  &= (2e, 2k+\varphi(e,e))
\end{align*}
It looks like: $(e,k)^n = (ne, nk + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\varphi(e,e))$, so lets check this inductively.\\
Certainly, it holds for $n=1,2$, so lets assume it holds for $n=t$ and show that it must then hold for $n=t+1$.
\begin{align*}
  (e,k)^{(t+1)} &= (e,k)^t.(e,k) \\
  &= (te, tk+ (t-1)\varphi(e,e)).(e,k) \\
  &= ((t+1)e, (t+1)k+ \frac{t(t-1)}{2}\varphi(e,e) + \varphi(te,e)) \\
  &= ((t+1)e, (t+1)k+ \frac{t(t-1)}{2}\varphi(e,e) + t\varphi(e,e)) \\
  &= ((t+1)e, (t+1)k+ \frac{t(t+1)}{2}\varphi(e,e)) \\
\end{align*}
The induction hypothesis holds, hence: $(e,k)^{t} = (te, tk+ \frac{t(t-1)}{2}\varphi(e,e)), \; t \ge 1$.\\
\\
Hence, when $t=p$ (an odd prime) we have that $p(p-1)/2$ is a multiple of $p$ from which we determine that:
$$(e,k)^p = (pe,pk + \frac{p(p-1)}{2}\varphi(e,e)) = (0,0)$$
since we know that: $pe=0, \; pk=0, \; \frac{p(p-1)}{2}\varphi(e,e)=0$, and $(0,0)$ is the identity for $G$.\\
\\
If $p=2$, then $(e,k)^2 = (2e, 2k + \varphi(e,e)) = (0,\varphi(e,e))$.  Then:
\begin{align*}
  (e,k)^4 &= [(e,k)^2]^2 \\
  &= (0,\varphi(e,e))^2 \\
  &= (0,2 \varphi(e,e) + \varphi(0,0)) \\
  &= (0,0) \tag{D.5}
\end{align*}
(D.5) follows from $2 \varphi(e,e)=0$ and $\varphi(0,0)=0$ which follows from (D.3)/(D.4).
Hence, in the case $p=2$ we have that $(e,k)^4 = (0,0)$.\\
